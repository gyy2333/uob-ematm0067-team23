\begin{abstract}
This project examines the evolution of scientific research
in Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML),
and Natural Language Processing (NLP) by analysing a large
corpus of scientific abstracts spanning 1991--2021. The
primary goal is to understand how research focus, language,
and communication methods have shifted across time periods,
and to evaluate how different text analytics techniques
affect the insights that can be derived.

We developed a text analysis pipeline covering preprocessing,
temporal segmentation, multiple text representations
(TF-IDF unigrams, n-gram TF-IDF, and BERT embeddings), and
analytical methods including keyword analysis, LDA, BERTopic,
and dimensionality reduction via Truncated SVD and t-SNE.
The corpus was segmented into four periods (1991--1998,
1999--2006, 2007--2014, and 2015--2021) to identify
longitudinal patterns across \texttt{cs.AI}, \texttt{cs.LG},
and \texttt{cs.CL} subfields.

Our findings show distinct temporal shifts in research
language and focus, with greater specialisation and the
emergence of complex multi-word technical terms in later
periods. TF-IDF and n-gram keyword analysis most reliably
captured paradigm shifts. BERTopic revealed that the modern
era is defined by thematic diversification rather than
concentration around a single topic. LDA surfaced coherent
latent themes but proved sensitive to vocabulary drift.
BERT embeddings organised papers by topic rather than era,
representing a useful negative finding for diachronic
analysis. The work highlights the trade-off between
interpretability and semantic depth across methods, and
demonstrates that combining diverse analytical approaches
yields a more complete picture of how research has evolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The swift advancement of research in Artificial Intelligence
(AI), Machine Learning (ML), and Natural Language Processing
(NLP) has resulted in major transformations in the way
scientific work is structured, communicated, and has evolved
over time. Scientific abstracts, serving as concise summaries
of research findings, offer a rich source of information for
understanding these changes. Examining abstracts across
different time periods can reveal patterns in research
emphasis, vocabulary use, and methodological preferences
\citep{arxiv2020}.

Understanding these shifts matters for several reasons. It
allows researchers to recognise changes in dominant
paradigms — such as the transition from rule-based approaches
to data-driven strategies and the rise of deep learning
\citep{devlin-etal-2019-bert} — and provides insight into
how the complexity and style of scientific communication
evolve as a discipline matures.

This project analyses a large corpus of scientific abstracts
sourced from the arXiv dataset available via HuggingFace
\citep{arxiv2020}, focusing specifically on three subject
categories: \texttt{cs.AI} (Artificial Intelligence),
\texttt{cs.LG} (Machine Learning), and \texttt{cs.CL}
(Computation and Language / NLP). The dataset contains
metadata including titles, categories, and publication
identifiers, from which temporal information was extracted.
The corpus covers four time periods spanning 1991--2021,
enabling a longitudinal investigation of research
development: 1991--1998 (symbolic AI era), 1999--2006
(statistical learning era), 2007--2014 (deep learning
emergence), and 2015--2021 (transformer era).

A key difficulty in this task is that language evolves over
time. Terminology changes, new concepts emerge, and the same
ideas may be expressed differently across eras. The dataset
also exhibits significant imbalance across periods, with
recent years containing far more publications than earlier
ones. These factors complicate direct comparison and
necessitate careful preprocessing and normalisation
\citep{scikit-learn, nltk}.

To illustrate this concretely, consider the vocabulary
shift visible even qualitatively: an abstract from the
early 1990s might reference expert systems, knowledge bases,
or logic programming, while a recent abstract from 2015--2021
is more likely to discuss pre-trained transformers
\citep{devlin-etal-2019-bert}, attention mechanisms, or
convolutional neural networks.

To address these challenges, this project constructs a
systematic text analysis pipeline and evaluates multiple
analytical methods. The goal is not only to identify
patterns in the data but also to understand how different
text representation and comparison techniques affect the
insights that can be derived. We explore two experimental
axes. The first is \textbf{text representation}: we compare
TF-IDF unigrams \citep{scikit-learn}, n-gram TF-IDF
(bigrams and trigrams), and contextual BERT embeddings
\citep{bge2023}. The second is \textbf{topic discovery
method}: we compare TF-IDF keyword analysis, Latent
Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) \citep{blei2003latent}, and
BERTopic \citep{grootendorst2022bertopic}. We additionally
apply Truncated SVD \citep{scikit-learn} to trace the
vocabulary trajectory of the field and t-SNE
\citep{vandermaaten2008} to visualise how BERT embeddings
distribute across periods.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the full pipeline.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} presents results and
evaluation. We conclude in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data Preparation}

The corpus was loaded from the arXiv HuggingFace dataset
\citep{arxiv2020} and filtered to \texttt{cs.AI},
\texttt{cs.LG}, and \texttt{cs.CL}. Unnecessary columns
were removed, abstracts with missing text were dropped, and
category labels were standardised. Papers were then assigned
to one of four time periods: 1991--1998, 1999--2006,
2007--2014, and 2015--2021. A pronounced class imbalance
exists across periods: Period 4 contains far more papers
than earlier periods, reflecting the exponential growth of
the field after 2012. To ensure fair comparisons, all
keyword and topic scores were normalised rather than using
raw counts.

\subsection{Text Preprocessing}

All abstracts went through the following pipeline:
lowercasing, removal of special characters and digits,
tokenisation, stopword removal using the combined
scikit-learn and NLTK English stopword lists
\citep{scikit-learn, nltk}, and lemmatisation using
WordNet with part-of-speech (POS) tagging to accurately
restore verbs, nouns, and adjectives to their root forms.
POS tags were mapped to WordNet categories prior to
lemmatisation to improve accuracy over tag-agnostic
approaches. A custom domain stopword list was additionally
applied to suppress high-frequency but uninformative
academic terms such as ``paper'', ``proposed'', and
``results'', which would otherwise dominate TF-IDF scores
without providing era-discriminative signal.

\subsection{Analytic Axis 1: Text Representation}
\label{subsec:representation}

We compare three text representations, each offering a
different trade-off between interpretability and semantic
richness.

\paragraph{TF-IDF Unigrams (Baseline).}
TF-IDF weights each word by its frequency in a document
relative to the corpus, producing a sparse vector where
each dimension maps directly to a human-readable word.
\textit{Hypothesis: Unigram TF-IDF will identify
distinctive keywords per period but will miss multi-word
methodological phrases and semantically related terms.}

\paragraph{N-gram TF-IDF.}
We extend TF-IDF to bigrams and trigrams (range 2--3)
using \texttt{CountVectorizer} \citep{scikit-learn},
capturing compound phrases like ``convolutional neural
network'' that unigram analysis splits. N-gram density
is also tracked as a proxy for the technical specificity
of writing over time.
\textit{Hypothesis: N-gram TF-IDF will reveal
method-specific vocabulary shifts more clearly than
unigrams, at the cost of a larger, sparser feature space.}

\paragraph{BERT Embeddings.}
We use the \texttt{BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5} model
\citep{bge2023} from HuggingFace to produce dense
768-dimensional embeddings per abstract. This model was
selected for its strong semantic capture ability and
performance on academic text. Embeddings are computed as
the mean-pooled final hidden layer over all tokens.
\textit{Hypothesis: BERT will capture semantic similarity
effectively but may be less sensitive to the
vocabulary-level paradigm shifts that characterise era
transitions.}

\subsection{Analytic Axis 2: Topic Discovery Method}
\label{subsec:comparison}

\paragraph{TF-IDF Keyword Analysis (Baseline).}
For each period and category we extract the top-$k$ terms
by normalised TF-IDF score at both unigram and n-gram
levels, to identify concepts gaining or losing prominence.
\textit{Hypothesis: Keyword analysis will be the most
interpretable approach but may miss latent themes spanning
multiple vocabulary items.}

\paragraph{Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA).}
LDA \citep{blei2003latent} models each document as a mixture
of latent topics and each topic as a word distribution.
We apply it to the preprocessed corpus to discover recurring
thematic clusters without requiring labelled data, then
track topic prevalence across periods.
\textit{Hypothesis: LDA will complement keyword analysis
by grouping co-occurring terms into coherent themes, though
its bag-of-words assumption may limit sensitivity to rapidly
evolving technical vocabulary.}

\paragraph{BERTopic.}
BERTopic \citep{grootendorst2022bertopic} clusters documents
by their BERT embeddings and labels each cluster using
class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF). We customise the pipeline
using UMAP \citep{mcinnes2018umap} for dimensionality
reduction based on semantic relationships, and HDBSCAN
\citep{campello2013hdbscan} for density-based clustering
using Euclidean distance, chosen to produce robust,
non-fragmented topic clusters. Unigrams and bigrams are
retained for topic word extraction. Topic intensity ---
the relative frequency of a topic within a period and
category --- is tracked over time to reveal how research
themes evolve across subfields.
\textit{Hypothesis: BERTopic will produce more semantically
coherent topics than LDA, particularly for concepts that
share meaning but vary in surface form across eras, due
to its embedding-based clustering rather than bag-of-words
assumptions.}

\subsection{Temporal Drift Visualisation}

\textbf{Truncated SVD} \citep{scikit-learn} reduces the
TF-IDF matrix to 2D; period centroids are connected to
trace the field's macroscopic vocabulary trajectory, with
axis labels derived from the most extreme terms at each
pole. \textbf{t-SNE} \citep{vandermaaten2008} is applied
to BERT embeddings on a random sample of 5,000 abstracts
(perplexity 45) to assess whether papers cluster by time
period in semantic space.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Discussion}

The custom stopword list required manual judgement: terms
like ``learn'' and ``network'' carry genuine discriminative
signal but their extreme frequency in Period 4 may mask
subtler thematic patterns. This directly affected LDA —
the term ``learn'' saturates all five topic-word
distributions, reducing boundary sharpness between topics.
A stricter stopword policy or larger vocabulary
($>1{,}000$ features) would likely produce cleaner topic
separation. BERTopic's intensity collapse in 2015--2021
confirms diversification but cannot describe the content
of that diversity without examining full topic label lists.
The t-SNE visualisation is affected by class imbalance —
Period 4 dominates the 5,000-abstract sample — meaning
earlier periods are underrepresented and harder to
interpret geometrically.

Despite these limitations, findings are highly consistent
across methods. TF-IDF keyword and n-gram analysis produced
the most interpretable and robust outputs. BERTopic added
a complementary view of thematic diversification. LDA
provided interpretable topics but showed less stability
across hyperparameter choices. BERT embeddings were least
effective for era-level temporal analysis. The main
limitation of the overall approach is treating categories
as static and mutually exclusive, whereas in practice many
papers span subfields and period transitions are gradual.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This work applied a multi-method text analytics pipeline to
30 years of arXiv abstracts from AI, ML, and NLP subfields.
Across all methods, the findings tell a consistent story:
the corpus transitions from symbolic AI and formal methods
in the 1990s, through probabilistic and statistical learning
in the 2000s, into neural methods from 2007, and finally
into a highly diverse, transformer-dominated landscape from
2015.

TF-IDF keyword and n-gram analysis were the most effective
tools for detecting this transition. Multi-word phrases
such as \textit{convolutional neural}, \textit{pre train},
and \textit{reinforcement learn} were essential signals
invisible to unigram analysis. BERTopic added a distinct
insight: the modern era is characterised by the
fragmentation of research into many specialised directions,
reflected in sharply declining per-topic intensity after
2014. LDA surfaced interpretable thematic groupings that
broadly aligned with keyword trends, though it was more
sensitive to hyperparameter choices than BERTopic.

BERT embeddings did not cluster by time period, which is a
useful negative result. Semantic representations encode
research content at the topic level rather than the era
level, making TF-IDF-based representations more appropriate
